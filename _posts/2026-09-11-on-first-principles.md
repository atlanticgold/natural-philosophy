Every inquiry begins somewhere, and the choice of where to begin is already a philosophical commitment. To ask "what is this, really?" is to assume that the thing in question has a nature independent of how it is currently described, categorized, or used. That assumption is not obvious. It has to be earned.

A first principle, in the old sense, is not a slogan or an axiom chosen for convenience. It is the point past which a chain of "why" questions cannot usefully be pushed further within a given inquiry — not because the questioning becomes impossible, but because it changes subject. Ask why water boils at a given temperature and you arrive, eventually, at facts about molecular motion. Ask why those facts hold and you have left chemistry for physics. Ask why *those* laws hold and you may have left physics altogether.

> The person who claims to reason from first principles has usually just stopped asking "why" at a point that happens to suit them.

This is not a criticism of the method so much as a warning about its abuse. Reasoning from first principles is only as good as the honesty with which the principles were chosen. It is easy to dress up an assumption as a foundation.

## What this journal is for

These essays will not try to settle such questions. They are exercises in looking closely at ordinary things — causation, identity, measurement, kinds — until their apparent obviousness gives way to something worth examining. That is the whole method: patience, applied to what is usually taken for granted.

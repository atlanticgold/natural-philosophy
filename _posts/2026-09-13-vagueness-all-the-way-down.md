Take a heap of sand and remove one grain. It is still a heap. Remove another. Still a heap. Repeat the process a hundred thousand times, one grain at a time, and at no single step does a heap become a non-heap — yet a single grain of sand is not a heap. Somewhere in that process the predicate "is a heap" stopped applying, without ever visibly failing to apply.

This is not a puzzle about sand. It is a puzzle about almost every predicate ordinary language relies on: *tall*, *bald*, *red*, *rich*, *late*. Each admits of borderline cases that no amount of careful observation resolves, because the trouble is not in the observing.

The threshold model developed for natural kinds applies here with almost no modification. A heap, on that model, is whatever falls within some distance of a prototype along the relevant dimension — grain count, in this case — and the sorites paradox is just what it looks like to walk slowly across a threshold whose exact location was never determinate to begin with. Vagueness is not a special defect of the predicate "heap"; it is the generic behavior of any threshold-based category examined one small step at a time.

That has a consequence for the idea of a first principle as a point past which "why" questions cannot usefully be pushed further. If most of the concepts inquiry is built from are themselves threshold concepts with no sharp edge, then the "stopping point" of a chain of justification is unlikely to be a sharp edge either. It will be a region where further questioning stops paying for itself, not a foundation in the sense of something with a determinate boundary underneath it. The honesty asked for there — noticing when you have simply stopped asking "why" at a convenient point — turns out to be even harder than it first appeared, because there may be no fact of the matter about where the convenient point ends and the principled one begins.

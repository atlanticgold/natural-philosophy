To see a chair is, on one very old view, to perform a small inference: raw sensory data comes in, and the mind supplies what the data leaves out — depth, solidity, the fact that the thing affords sitting. J.J. Gibson's ecological approach to perception denies this almost entirely. On his account, the information needed to perceive a chair as a chair is already structured in the light reaching the eye; perceiving is a form of *picking up* that information directly, not inferring it from something poorer.[^1]

[^1]: This is easy to caricature as "perception involves no processing at all," which is not quite the claim. The claim is narrower: that the relevant structure is already present in the optic array, so explaining ordinary perception does not require positing an intermediate stage of unconscious inference from impoverished data.

The most contested piece of this picture is the *affordance* — the idea that a chair does not merely look chair-shaped, but is perceived as sit-on-able, directly, the way a gap in a fence is perceived as passable or not (Gibson 1979). Critics have objected that this smuggles back in exactly the kind of interpretive work Gibson wants to avoid: knowing that something affords sitting seems to require knowing quite a lot about bodies, weight, and rest, and it is not obvious that this knowledge can simply be read off the light.

That objection has a familiar shape. It is, in effect, a boundary dispute: where does "directly perceived" end and "inferred" begin? This site's essay on [natural kinds]({% post_url 2026-09-12-kinds-and-their-boundaries %}) makes a parallel argument about categories generally — that a boundary can be real without being sharp, and that the demand for a sharp line is often doing more work in an objection than it should. An affordance may be exactly this kind of graded case: more directly perceived for a spider crossing a familiar web, less so for a person sizing up an unfamiliar chair in the dark.

None of this settles the dispute between ecological and inferential theories of perception, which has run for decades without a clear winner. But it suggests the dispute may partly be about how much sharpness a theory of perception is allowed to promise, rather than about perception alone.
